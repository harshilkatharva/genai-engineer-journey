CONSUMER CORPUS FOR EMBEDDING API COST AND LATENCY BENCHMARK
Synthetic benchmark corpus — approximately 20–25K tokens

Purpose:
This corpus is designed for testing embedding API cost and latency. For a 100-document
benchmark, split the corpus into 100 passages/documents, send them to the embedding API,
and record request latency and token usage. The content is synthetic consumer-domain
material covering shopping, payments, services, travel, food, electronics, support,
recommendations, logistics, reviews, privacy, accessibility, and research.

Suggested measurements:
- total API latency
- average latency per document
- input token count
- cost per request
- cost per 100 documents
- throughput (documents/second)

The content is synthetic and intended for benchmarking.

## Shopping and Product Discovery
Consumers increasingly begin shopping journeys online, comparing products, prices, reviews, specifications, warranties, delivery estimates, and return policies. A useful consumer platform makes discovery easy without overwhelming people with repetitive choices. Product pages should clearly separate essential information from optional details. Shoppers often want to know what a product does, whether it fits their needs, how durable it is, what other customers experienced, and what the total cost will be after shipping, taxes, discounts, and subscriptions.

## Payments, Pricing, and Budgeting
Consumers evaluate purchases using more than the headline price. The final amount can include shipping, taxes, installation, service charges, financing costs, and optional protection plans. A transparent checkout should show these components before the customer commits. Budgeting tools can help households organize recurring expenses such as utilities, internet access, mobile plans, groceries, transportation, insurance, entertainment, and subscriptions.

## Home and Household Services
Household services include cleaning, repairs, maintenance, moving assistance, installation, appliance servicing, pest control, landscaping, and recurring or one-time jobs. Consumers usually want to know the provider's service area, available times, estimated cost, cancellation terms, and what is included in the quoted price. Clear service descriptions reduce misunderstandings between customers and providers.

## Travel and Local Experiences
Travel planning combines transportation, accommodation, food, activities, weather, local mobility, and personal preferences. A traveler may compare destinations based on budget, duration, accessibility, season, and desired pace. Accommodation decisions often involve location, room configuration, cleanliness, cancellation policies, amenities, breakfast options, and total nightly cost.

## Food, Groceries, and Meal Planning
Grocery shopping involves staples, fresh foods, household supplies, snacks, beverages, and ingredients for planned meals. Consumers may care about price, brand, package size, nutrition information, dietary preferences, preparation time, storage life, and availability. Meal planning can reduce repeated decision-making by organizing meals, quantities, leftovers, substitutions, and a consolidated shopping list.

## Consumer Electronics and Digital Products
Electronics shoppers compare devices based on performance, battery life, screen quality, storage, connectivity, durability, software support, accessories, repairability, and price. Compatibility is a common source of consumer frustration because devices may require specific connectors, operating systems, wireless standards, applications, or account types.

## Customer Support and Returns
Customer support handles delivery questions, damaged items, missing components, account problems, billing discrepancies, warranty claims, and return requests. Support systems can reduce frustration by preserving conversation context. Return policies should clearly explain eligibility windows, condition requirements, excluded categories, return shipping responsibilities, refunds, exchanges, and applicable charges.

## Personalization and Recommendations
Personalization can make large consumer catalogs easier to navigate. Useful signals include stated preferences, previous purchases, saved products, viewed categories, price ranges, and explicit feedback. Recommendation quality can be evaluated using relevance, diversity, novelty, and customer satisfaction. Customers should be able to adjust important preferences and override recommendations.

## Delivery, Logistics, and Availability
Delivery is part of the product experience because customers care about when and how an item will arrive. Useful information includes estimated delivery date, shipping method, package tracking, delivery fees, pickup options, and restrictions. Inventory information is time-sensitive, so systems should communicate uncertainty rather than presenting an estimate as a guarantee.

## Reviews and Consumer Trust
Reviews help customers understand real-world product experiences. Useful review systems preserve context such as purchase verification, usage period, product variant, and category. Review summaries can identify recurring themes such as durability, fit, setup difficulty, noise, packaging, or customer support. Platforms should detect obvious spam and duplicate submissions while recognizing that unusual opinions are not necessarily fraudulent.

## Privacy, Security, and Consumer Controls
Consumer applications handle account details, purchase history, preferences, addresses, payment metadata, support conversations, and device activity. Customers should understand what information is collected and why it is needed. Security practices include authentication, session management, protection against unauthorized account access, secure payment handling, and monitoring for suspicious activity.

## Accessibility and Inclusive Consumer Design
Consumer services should work for people with different abilities, devices, languages, and levels of technical familiarity. Accessible design includes readable typography, sufficient contrast, keyboard navigation, meaningful labels, captions, alternative text, and predictable interaction patterns. Forms should minimize unnecessary fields and provide clear error messages.

## Consumer Research and Feedback
Consumer research helps organizations understand why customers succeed or struggle with a product. Surveys, interviews, usability tests, support conversations, reviews, and behavioral analytics each reveal different aspects of the experience. Quantitative data can show where a problem occurs, while qualitative feedback can explain why.

## Consumer Topic 1: Mobile Shopping
mobile shopping affects how consumers evaluate convenience, value, trust, and control. Customers compare alternatives according to urgency, budget, previous experience, household needs, and product category. A useful consumer service makes these differences visible without requiring customers to become experts.

For mobile shopping, useful information supports a concrete decision. Customers may compare total cost, availability, compatibility, quality, delivery timing, service terms, and expected effort. Interfaces should distinguish facts from estimates and make important constraints easy to discover. If information is missing, the system should say so rather than silently filling the gap with an assumption.

Organizations can improve mobile shopping by collecting feedback, examining search and purchase behavior, and testing alternative experiences. Metrics should be interpreted with context. A high conversion rate may coexist with poor long-term satisfaction, while a low interaction rate may simply mean that customers found the answer quickly. Consumer outcomes should therefore be evaluated using multiple measures.

Clear communication remains important throughout the journey. Whether a customer is discovering a product, paying for an order, waiting for delivery, requesting support, or deciding whether to return an item, the service should provide an understandable next step. Good consumer design reduces uncertainty while preserving customer choice.
